How Much Does A Whole-Home Repipe Cost?

The honest answer is a range that depends on real, checkable factors, not a single number. Here's what actually drives the cost up or down.

A whole-home repipe replaces the interior supply lines throughout a house rather than patching individual leaks as they occur, and the total cost genuinely varies based on home size, material choice, and how much of the plumbing is accessible without opening finished walls. Giving a single flat number without seeing the home would be misleading, but the real factors that move the price are worth understanding before you get a quote.

The Biggest Cost Factor

Home Size And Fixture Count

Square footage and the number of bathrooms, kitchens, and water-using appliances set the baseline scope, since each fixture needs its own supply run connected back to the main system. A single-bathroom home with a basic kitchen costs meaningfully less to repipe than a larger home with multiple bathrooms, a wet bar, and a laundry room, simply because there's more total pipe to run.

Material Choice

PEX Vs. Copper

PEX tubing is flexible, requires fewer fittings, and typically costs less installed than rigid copper for the same scope, largely because it can be run through existing stud bays and chases with fewer wall openings needed. Copper has a longer historical track record and some homeowners prefer it for that reason, but it costs more in both material and labor given the additional fittings and joints a rigid pipe system requires.

Wall And Floor Access

Why This Can Change The Price Significantly

A home with an open, unfinished basement or accessible crawlspace allows most new pipe runs to be completed without opening finished walls at all, which meaningfully reduces both the repiping cost and the drywall patching that follows. A home on a slab foundation, or one with entirely finished walls and no accessible chase points, requires more wall openings and more finish-work repair afterward, which adds real cost beyond the plumbing itself.

Questions To Ask When Getting A Quote

  • Does the quote include drywall patching, or is that a separate cost?
  • Is the quote based on PEX, copper, or a mix, and why?
  • Will the water be shut off in sections, or for the entire project duration?
  • Is a permit and inspection included in the quoted price?
When A Repipe Actually Makes Sense

The Real Trigger Point

Repiping is the right call when leaks keep appearing at new spots after each individual repair, which signals the pipe material itself, usually original galvanized steel, has reached the end of its practical life rather than one section simply failing. This comes up often in older housing stock like Elwood's, where much of the construction predates World War II, and it's a genuinely common request in mixed-era towns like Lapel for the 1960s-built section of that community specifically.

If you're not yet sure whether repair or full repiping is the right call, a straightforward way to check is whether you've needed more than one pipe-related repair in the same house within the past year or two. Water heater age matters here too, since our water heater replacement team often finds scale-related tank failure alongside aging supply lines on the same older-home service call.

Financing And Timing

Making A Big Project More Manageable

A whole-home repipe is a genuinely significant expense, and it's reasonable to weigh timing against other planned home projects. If a kitchen or bathroom remodel is already planned, coordinating the repipe with that project can reduce total drywall and finish-work cost, since walls being opened for the remodel anyway don't need a second separate opening for plumbing access.

Getting a written, itemized quote that separates plumbing labor, materials, permit fees, and drywall restoration lets you compare quotes accurately and understand exactly what you're paying for at each stage of the project.

Comparing Quotes Fairly

Why The Lowest Number Isn't Always The Best Deal

A significantly lower quote sometimes reflects a real difference in scope rather than better pricing for the same work, a quote that excludes drywall restoration, permit fees, or uses a cheaper fitting standard can look attractive on paper while leaving real costs for you to absorb later. Asking each contractor to itemize the same categories, material, labor, permit, and finish work, makes an apples-to-apples comparison actually possible.

A confirmed price that changes only if you request added scope, not one that grows once work has already started, is the standard worth expecting from any repiping quote regardless of which contractor you choose, and it's a fair question to ask directly before signing anything.
