Toilet Won't Stop Running? Here's What To Check

A running toilet is almost always one of two parts failing, and identifying which one changes whether this is a five-minute fix or a same-day call.

A toilet that runs continuously, or that cycles on briefly every few minutes even when no one has flushed it, is wasting water the entire time it's happening, and that adds up meaningfully on a metered water bill over even a single billing cycle. The good news is that the cause is almost always one of two parts, both inside the tank, and neither requires replacing the toilet.

The Two Most Likely Causes

Flapper Vs. Fill Valve

The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank that lifts when you flush and drops back down to seal the tank. A worn, warped, or misaligned flapper doesn't seal completely, letting water slowly leak from the tank into the bowl, which the fill valve then tries to compensate for by periodically topping the tank back off. That brief, intermittent running sound is usually a flapper problem.

The fill valve itself controls how much water enters the tank after a flush and shuts off at the correct level. A fill valve that doesn't shut off completely causes continuous running rather than the intermittent cycling a bad flapper produces. Listening for whether the sound is constant or comes in short bursts is a useful first clue to which part is actually failing.

A Quick Check Before Calling

  • Remove the tank lid and watch the flapper after a flush: does it seal fully, or does it stay slightly open?
  • Check whether water is at or above the overflow tube's top, a sign the fill valve isn't shutting off correctly.
  • Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank; color appearing in the bowl without flushing confirms a flapper leak.
When It's Not Either Of Those

Less Common But Still Repairable Causes

Occasionally the issue is a cracked overflow tube, a fill valve set too high so it constantly overflows into the tube, or a warped flush valve seat that a new flapper won't seal against no matter how well-fitted. These are still straightforward repairs covered under our toilet repair and installation service, but they're less obvious without opening the tank and checking each component individually.

A toilet that's original to a home built decades ago, common in mixed-era towns like Lapel, sometimes has tank hardware that's simply worn out across the board rather than one isolated failed part. In that case, replacing the fill valve and flapper together resolves it more reliably than fixing one and waiting to see if the other fails next. A Word On Toilet Tank Tablets

Why We Don't Recommend Drop-In Cleaners

Chlorine-based drop-in tank tablets, marketed to keep a toilet bowl clean between scrubs, sit in the tank continuously exposing the flapper and other rubber components to a low but constant level of chlorine. Over months, that exposure degrades rubber seals faster than normal use alone, which means a tank tablet habit can genuinely be part of why a flapper needed replacing sooner than expected.

If bowl cleanliness between scrubs is the actual goal, a bowl-specific cleaner used during a normal cleaning routine accomplishes the same thing without leaving anything sitting in the tank long-term.

When Replacement Makes More Sense

Repeated Repairs Vs. A New Toilet

A toilet that's needed the flapper, fill valve, and flush valve seat replaced individually over a couple of years is often a candidate for full replacement rather than another round of parts. Beyond the cumulative repair cost, a toilet original to a home built before 1994, when the federal Energy Policy Act's 1.6-gallon-per-flush standard took effect, uses considerably more water per flush than a modern low-flow model, so replacement can pay for itself over time through lower water bills alone.

A cracked tank or bowl is the clearest signal that repair has reached its limit, since a crack, even a hairline one, tends to worsen with the normal expansion and contraction of daily use and can eventually fail suddenly rather than gradually.

What This Actually Costs You

The Real Water Waste

A continuously running toilet can waste hundreds of gallons in a single day depending on how poorly the flapper seals, which is a real, calculable cost on a metered water bill, not a minor inconvenience. Catching it within days of it starting, rather than weeks, is the practical difference between a normal bill and a noticeably inflated one.
