Buying An Older Home? Get A Sewer Camera Inspection First

A standard home inspection doesn't check the sewer lateral. For an older home, that's a real gap worth closing before you close on the house.

A standard real estate home inspection covers a lot of ground, the roof, the foundation, electrical systems, visible plumbing fixtures, but it typically does not include the sewer lateral, the underground line connecting the house to the municipal sewer main or an on-site septic system. For a newer home, that gap rarely matters. For an older home, it's a real, checkable blind spot worth closing before you sign anything.

Why This Gap Exists

The Sewer Line Is Genuinely Out Of Sight

A general home inspector checks what's visible and accessible without specialized equipment, and a buried sewer lateral simply isn't part of that scope in most standard inspections. Unless a buyer specifically requests a separate sewer camera inspection, a line with real problems, root intrusion, a cracked joint, or a section nearing the end of its material life, can pass the overall home inspection cleanly. It can then become an expensive surprise for the new owner within the first year of ownership.

Why Older Homes Are The Real Risk

Pipe Materials That Reach The End Of Their Life

Original clay sewer laterals, common in homes built before the 1970s across Madison County, are genuinely prone to root intrusion at pipe joints, a slow process that develops over decades and often shows no symptoms until it's fairly advanced. Some homes built in certain eras used Orangeburg pipe, a bitumen-impregnated fiber pipe with a limited practical service life regardless of how well it's been maintained, which is worth specifically identifying if present.

This is a real consideration in mixed-era towns like Lapel, where the historic Fishersburg area and other older sections predate the town's main 1960s and 2000s building waves. The same applies to any home across Anderson's own older neighborhoods where original clay or cast iron drain lines are still in service.

When To Request This Before Closing

  • The home was built before 1970, or you don't know the sewer line's material or age.
  • Mature trees are near the home's likely sewer line path.
  • The seller mentions any past drain backup or slow-drain history, even if described as resolved.
  • You're buying in an area known for older clay or original cast iron plumbing.
What The Inspection Actually Shows

Real Evidence, Not A Guess

Our sewer camera inspection feeds a waterproof camera through the line and provides recorded footage, showing exactly what condition the pipe is in rather than relying on assumptions based on the home's age alone. That footage becomes real documentation you can use in negotiations if a problem is found, or simply useful confirmation to act on if the line is sound.

If a problem is found, understanding whether it's a localized issue our sewer line repair service can address with a spot repair, versus something requiring full replacement, changes both the negotiating conversation and the actual cost you're weighing against the purchase.

Timing It Right

When To Schedule This In The Buying Process

Requesting a sewer camera inspection during your home inspection contingency period, alongside the general home inspection, keeps the timeline aligned with your other negotiating position. Scheduling it after your inspection contingency has already closed removes your ability to negotiate based on what it finds, so timing genuinely matters here, not just whether you get the inspection at all.

For sellers, having a clean sewer inspection report ready ahead of listing can also prevent the issue from becoming a late, disruptive negotiating point once a buyer's own inspector raises it during their contingency period. Either way, treating the sewer line as a real, checkable part of the purchase decision, rather than an assumed extension of the general home inspection, is the practical takeaway here regardless of which side of the transaction you're on.

What A Clean Report Is Worth

Confirmation Has A Real Value Too

Not every inspection finds a problem, and a confirmed clean sewer line is genuinely useful information on its own, not just a box to check. Knowing the line is sound removes one more variable from an already stressful home-buying process, and it's a small cost relative to the size of the overall purchase decision it informs. For an older home anywhere in Madison County, treating this as a standard part of due diligence, the same way you'd treat a roof or foundation check, is simply the more thorough and complete way to buy a house.
