What Causes Low Water Pressure Throughout The House?

If every fixture is weak, not just one, the cause is upstream of your fixtures. Here's how to narrow it down before calling anyone.

The first useful thing to determine is whether low pressure affects one fixture or the whole house. A single weak faucet or showerhead usually means a clogged aerator or fixture-specific mineral buildup, an easy fix. Every fixture running weak at once points to something shared: the main shutoff valve, the service line, or a system-wide issue affecting water before it ever reaches an individual tap.

Check The Easy Things First

Valves And Filters

A partially closed main shutoff valve or meter valve is worth ruling out first, since it costs nothing to check and is a surprisingly common cause after any recent plumbing work, a water heater service, or even an accidental bump. On a ball-type valve, the handle should sit fully parallel with the pipe when open; anything less than that restricts flow.

If your home has a whole-house sediment filter or a water softener on the main line, a clogged filter cartridge or an overdue softener resin bed can also cause a system-wide pressure drop. Checking when either was last serviced is a reasonable next step before assuming a bigger problem.

When It's Inside The Pipes

Corrosion And Scale Buildup

Older galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out, and the resulting mineral deposits narrow the interior diameter over decades, gradually reducing flow throughout the house even without any single point of failure. This is a real, common pattern in Anderson's older housing and even more pronounced in older towns like Alexandria, where much of the housing stock predates 1950 and hasn't been repiped since.

Unlike a valve or filter issue, corroded pipe doesn't resolve with a quick fix, the practical solution is repiping the affected sections, which is a bigger project but the only approach that actually restores pressure rather than working around the narrowed pipe.

When It's A Leak

Pressure Loss From An Escaping Line

A leak anywhere in the system, from a small pinhole in a wall to a larger service-line leak in the yard, reduces overall system pressure by giving water somewhere to escape besides your fixtures. If a pressure drop developed suddenly rather than gradually, and especially if it coincides with a rising water bill, leak detection should be the first step rather than assuming pipe age is the cause.

How To Narrow It Down Before Calling

  • Confirm it's whole-house, not one fixture, by testing a kitchen faucet, a bathroom faucet, and a shower separately.
  • Check whether both hot and cold water are affected, or just hot, which points toward the water heater specifically.
  • Check your main shutoff and meter valve to confirm both are fully open.
  • Note whether the drop was sudden or gradual, since that distinguishes a leak from age-related corrosion.
A Note On Pressure Regulators

The Component Most Homeowners Forget Exists

Many homes have a pressure-reducing valve installed where the service line enters the house, which steps down municipal supply pressure to a safer range for household plumbing. A failing regulator can drop pressure suddenly and uniformly across every fixture, mimicking the symptoms of a leak or corroded pipe without either actually being present. Since a regulator sits inline on the main supply, testing it accurately requires a pressure gauge and some plumbing knowledge, which is usually the point where a professional diagnosis is worth the cost of guessing wrong.

Replacing a failed regulator is a straightforward repair once confirmed under the Indiana Plumbing Code standard (675 IAC 16) for pressure-reducing valve installation, but confirming it correctly the first time saves both money and the frustration of replacing a part that wasn't actually the problem. A regulator set too low restricts pressure without any leak or corrosion involved at all, and adjusting or replacing it can resolve a whole-house complaint in a single visit once it's properly identified as the cause.

Municipal Supply Issues

When The Problem Isn't Inside Your House At All

Occasionally, low pressure originates outside the property entirely: a water main break nearby, scheduled utility maintenance, or high demand across a neighborhood during peak usage hours can all cause temporary pressure drops that no amount of internal plumbing work will fix. Checking with neighbors, or with Anderson Utilities directly, is a reasonable step if the pressure drop happened suddenly and affects more than just your own address.

If a municipal-side issue is ruled out and the problem persists at your address specifically, that confirms the cause is internal, and it's worth working through the checklist above systematically rather than guessing at a single likely culprit.
