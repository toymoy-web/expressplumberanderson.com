Signs Of A Slab Leak You Shouldn't Ignore

A slab leak develops out of sight, which is exactly why the early signs matter more than with a leak you can actually see.

A slab leak is a leak in a water line running underneath a home's concrete foundation, which makes it a genuinely different problem from a leak behind an accessible wall or in an open basement. There's no visible dripping to notice, no wet spot forming where you can see it directly, which means the early signs are indirect and easy to dismiss individually until they add up to a clear pattern.

The Warm Spot

Why This Is The Most Reliable Early Sign

A warm patch on an otherwise cool floor, especially over what would be a hot water line's likely path, is one of the more dependable early indicators of a slab leak. The escaping hot water warms the concrete directly above the break, and that warmth is often noticeable underfoot before any other symptom appears. It's worth paying attention to this even in a room where you wouldn't expect plumbing, since a line's actual path under the slab doesn't always match where fixtures are located above it.

The Water Bill And Meter Test

A Free Way To Confirm Something Is Actually Wrong

An unexplained rise in your water bill, with no corresponding change in household water use, is a strong signal that water is escaping somewhere in the system, slab leak or otherwise. The meter test costs nothing and takes a few minutes: shut off every fixture in the house, including anything on an automatic cycle like an ice maker or irrigation system, then check whether the water meter is still moving. If it is, water is escaping somewhere between the meter and your fixtures.

This test alone doesn't confirm a slab leak specifically, since the escaping water could be at the service line or another accessible location, but it does confirm that something is genuinely wrong rather than a bill anomaly, which is the first real decision point.

Signs Worth Investigating

  • A warm spot on the floor with no obvious source, especially over a suspected hot water line path.
  • An unexplained rise in your water bill without a change in usage.
  • The water meter still moving with every fixture in the house shut off.
  • The sound of running water audible through the floor with nothing actually running.
Why This Happens

The Real Cause Behind Most Slab Leaks

Most slab leaks develop from pinhole corrosion in copper supply lines, a slow, localized breakdown of the pipe wall that eventually creates a small leak point rather than a sudden failure. Heat accelerates this process, which is why slab leaks show up on hot water lines noticeably more often than cold ones. Soil conditions and water chemistry both play a role in how quickly this corrosion develops, which is part of why some homes in the same neighborhood see slab leaks and others don't despite similar age and construction.

Finding It Without Guessing

Electronic Detection Before Any Concrete Comes Up

Once a slab leak is suspected, the actual location has to be confirmed electronically before any concrete work starts. Thermal imaging shows the heat signature of the leaking hot water line, while acoustic equipment listens for the sound of pressurized water escaping through the slab. Together, these narrow the leak's location to within a foot or two, which is the difference between a targeted, minimal repair and unnecessary demolition based on a guess.

Our leak detection service covers this diagnostic step for any suspected hidden leak, not just slab leaks specifically, and it's always the first step before any repair recommendation. Once a slab leak is confirmed, our slab leak detection and repair service covers the repair itself, including the choice between a direct spot repair and rerouting the line above the slab entirely to avoid breaking concrete at all.

A Regional Note

Why Older Copper Plumbing Matters Here

Homes with original copper plumbing installed several decades ago are the most common candidates for slab leaks, since pinhole corrosion is a gradual process that takes years to develop into an actual leak. This shows up across Anderson's established neighborhoods and in older housing stock in towns like Elwood, where much of the construction dates to well before modern copper alloys and water treatment practices existed. A newer home with PEX supply lines is far less prone to this specific failure mode, since PEX doesn't corrode the way copper does.

What Happens Next

Repair Options Once The Leak Is Located

A direct spot repair opens a small, targeted section of concrete over the confirmed leak location, which is often the faster and less expensive option when the leak is in an accessible spot. Rerouting the line entirely through an attic or an accessible wall avoids breaking the slab at all, which is worth considering when the leak's location is difficult to access directly or when a homeowner would rather avoid concrete work in a finished space altogether. Neither option is universally better; the right choice depends on the leak's specific location and the home's layout.
