A Town Built For More People Than Live Here Now

Frankton's Boom-And-Bust History

Frankton was settled in 1829 by Jacob Sigler and laid out as a town in 1853, incorporating in 1871. Like Anderson and Elwood, Frankton grew fast during Indiana's natural gas boom in the late 1800s, when cheap fuel drew industry and new residents to towns across this part of the state. At its peak during that boom, Frankton's population reached roughly 2,500.

Unlike Anderson, which kept growing after the gas ran out, Frankton didn't. The population fell by about half once the boom ended, settling at today's figure of around 1,775. That matters for plumbing specifically: a meaningful share of the water and sewer infrastructure installed to serve a town of 2,500 is still the same infrastructure serving a town of 1,775 today, simply because a shrinking town rarely has the tax base to replace pipe ahead of failure the way a growing one does.

Frankton sits in Pipe Creek and Lafayette Townships in northern Madison County, with Pipe Creek itself running through the north side of town on its way to the White River. SR 128 passes through the town center, connecting east to SR 9 and west to SR 13.

Signs You Need A Plumber Here

What Shows Up On A Frankton Call

Original Gas-Boom-Era Supply Lines

Galvanized and early copper pipe from the 1880s-1900s construction wave is well past typical service life.

Oversized Original Infrastructure

Water and sewer lines sized for a bigger population than currently lives on them, which can affect pressure and flow differently than a correctly sized modern system.

Aging Cast Iron Drain Lines

Common source of root intrusion and reduced flow in homes from the boom-era construction period.

Deferred Maintenance On Older Homes

A smaller tax base and slower turnover mean original plumbing sometimes goes longer between updates than in a faster-growing town.

We also see a reasonable amount of newer infill construction in Frankton, built on lots within the original town plat rather than new subdivisions, so not every call here involves century-old pipe. Checking a home's actual construction era, rather than assuming based on the town's history, is still the right first step.
