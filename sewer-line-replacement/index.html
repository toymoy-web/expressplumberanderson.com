Replacement Vs. Repair

When The Whole Line Needs To Go

Replacement is a different job from repair: instead of fixing one damaged section, the entire lateral line from the house to the city connection or septic system is taken out and replaced. That's the right call when a camera inspection finds damage repeated along the line's length rather than one localized problem, or when the pipe material itself, like original Orangeburg pipe found in some older Indiana homes, has reached the end of its usable life.

We confirm with camera footage before recommending full replacement over a repair, since replacement is a bigger job and shouldn't be the default answer without that evidence.

Signs & Symptoms

Signs You Need Full Replacement, Not Repair

Damage At Multiple Points

Root intrusion or cracking repeated along the line's length, not one spot.

Original Orangeburg Or Clay Pipe

Aging pipe materials that have simply reached the end of their service life.

Line Has Collapsed

A fully collapsed section usually rules out a spot repair entirely.

Repeated Repairs Not Holding

When the same line needs repair again shortly after a previous fix.

Method

Trenchless Pipe Bursting Vs. Open Trench

Trenchless pipe bursting pulls a new pipe through the path of the old one, breaking the old pipe outward as it goes, which avoids trenching the entire yard and is our first option to evaluate on most Anderson lots. Open-trench replacement is still sometimes necessary when the line's path has shifted, when there isn't a clear straight run for the bursting head, or when other utilities cross too close to the line.

Before We Start Digging

  • We call Indiana 811 to have all underground utility lines marked before any digging.
  • Mark or point out any private lines you know about, such as an irrigation system or invisible fence.
  • Move vehicles and outdoor furniture away from the marked work area.
Permits

Permitting For A Full Replacement

A full sewer line replacement typically requires a permit and a final inspection, since the work involves the connection to the municipal sewer main or an on-site septic system, either of which Madison County treats as a regulated connection point rather than routine repair. We pull the permit and coordinate the inspection as part of the job rather than leaving that step to the homeowner.

Homes on a septic system rather than municipal sewer follow a related but separate permitting path through the county health department, since the connection point and inspection standard are different from a municipal tie-in, and we confirm which situation applies before scheduling the work.
