Two Eras Of Housing In One Town

Lapel's Real Housing Mix: 1960s And 2000s

Lapel was platted in 1876 when the railroad reached the area. The town is named for the coat-lapel shape the rail line traced across the countryside. Housing data shows Lapel's homes were primarily built in two distinct waves, the 1960s and the 2000s. That means we see genuinely different plumbing profiles on opposite sides of the same small town rather than one uniform housing era.

The historic Fishersburg area, an older community absorbed into Lapel's ZIP code, adds a third, even older layer to that mix. A service call here often starts with figuring out which era of construction we're actually dealing with before diagnosing the specific problem.

Lapel sits across Stony Creek and Green Townships and has grown steadily but modestly over the past two decades, unlike Pendleton's rapid expansion or Alexandria's long plateau. The town takes real pride in its history too, including the 1940 Lapel Bulldogs team that reached the state basketball Final Four. It's a point of local identity that longtime residents still bring up.

A quick way we sort out which era we're dealing with on a Lapel call, short of asking the homeowner directly, is checking the shutoff valve style and supply line material visible under a sink or at the water heater. 1960s-era gate valves and galvanized fittings look distinctly different from the ball valves and PEX common in 2000s construction.

Whichever part of Lapel a call comes from, dispatch runs around the clock rather than being limited to business hours. We ask for a rough construction year on every first call to a Lapel address specifically because the right diagnosis genuinely differs by decade here. A 1960s home's low water pressure usually means corroded galvanized supply lines. The same symptom in a 2000s-built home more often points to a pressure-reducing valve or a fixture-level restriction, not aging pipe at all. Whichever era, repair work follows the Indiana Plumbing Code standard (675 IAC 16).
